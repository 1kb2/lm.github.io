Seasonal note

October in the apiary

In the northern hemisphere the colony is shrinking and sealing itself in for winter.

  • The last forage

    Ivy in flower

    Ivy blooms from September into November and is often the last big nectar and pollen flow of the year. Ivy honey sets hard in the comb within weeks.

  • The colony

    Drones go out

    Workers stop feeding the males and push them out of the hive. Winter bees take over: they live four to six months instead of six weeks.

  • The beekeeper

    Check the stores

    A colony in a temperate climate needs roughly 15–20 kg of honey to last until spring. Mouse guards go on the entrance now.

The colony

Sixty thousand bodies, one animal

A summer honey bee colony holds 20,000 to 60,000 bees. Almost all of them are sterile female workers. Together they behave so much like a single organism that biologists call the colony a superorganism.

A worker's career

Five jobs in six weeks

Workers change roles as their glands mature. Ages are typical summer days after emerging from the cell, and colonies shift them when they need to.

    Waggle dance

    Directions, danced on the comb

    On the vertical comb inside the dark hive, "straight up" stands for the sun's direction. The angle of the waggle run tells the others which way to fly, and its length tells them how far. Karl von Frisch decoded it and shared a Nobel Prize for it in 1973.

    What the dancers read Fly 40° right of the sun for about 1.2 km. Waggle run ≈ 1.2 s, repeated with alternating return loops.

    The timing is a rule of thumb: about one second of waggling per kilometre. Below roughly 50–100 m, foragers do a round dance that carries no direction.

    Honey

    Nectar, minus most of the water

    Honey is flower nectar that bees have concentrated, enzymed and sealed in wax. The flower decides the colour and flavour; the bees decide the chemistry.

    The process

    From flower to capped cell

    1. Forage

      A forager sips nectar through her tongue into her honey stomach, visiting hundreds of flowers per trip.

    2. Add enzymes

      Invertase from her glands starts splitting sucrose into glucose and fructose while she flies home.

    3. Hand over

      House bees take the nectar mouth to mouth, a transfer called trophallaxis, and add more enzymes.

    4. Evaporate

      Nectar is spread thin in cells while bees fan their wings to drive the water off.

    5. Cap

      When water drops to about 17–18%, workers seal the cell with a lid of fresh wax.

    Water content

    Nectar

    At this concentration few microbes can grow, which is why sealed honey keeps for decades.

    Colour grading

    The Pfund scale

    Honey colour is graded in millimetres on a Pfund grader, which compares a wedge of honey against an amber glass standard. Lighter honeys tend to taste milder.

    Beyond honey

    What else the hive makes

    Beeswax

    Workers around 12–17 days old secrete it as flakes from glands on their abdomen. Bees eat several kilograms of honey to make one kilogram of wax.

    Propolis

    "Bee glue": tree resins mixed with wax. Bees use it to seal cracks and coat the inside of the hive, and it has antimicrobial properties.

    Royal jelly

    A protein-rich secretion from nurse bees' head glands. Every larva gets it for about three days; a future queen gets it for her whole development.

    Bee bread

    Pollen packed into cells with nectar and left to ferment. It is the colony's main protein source and feeds the nurse bees.

    In the kitchen

    Keeping honey well

    It set solid. Is it off?

    No. Crystallizing is natural and happens faster in glucose-rich honey like rapeseed. Stand the jar in warm water around 40 °C to make it runny again.

    Where to store it

    At room temperature, lid on tight. Honey pulls moisture from the air, and wet honey can ferment. Cool spots around 10–15 °C speed up crystallizing.

    Not for babies under 12 months

    Honey can carry spores of Clostridium botulinum, which an infant's gut can't yet handle. This applies to raw and pasteurized honey and to baked goods made with it.

    History

    Eight thousand years of sweetness

    People robbed wild nests long before anyone kept bees. The story runs from cliff faces to clay cylinders, straw skeps and the wooden box most beekeepers still use.

    1. c. 8,000 years ago
      Cuevas de la Araña, Valencia, Spain

      A honey hunter on a cave wall

      A rock painting shows a figure climbing ropes or vines to a wild nest, holding a basket, with bees flying around. It is one of the oldest known images of honey gathering.

    2. c. 5,500 years ago
      Borjomi, Georgia

      The oldest honey ever found

      Archaeologists found traces of honey in ceramic jars placed in a tomb, apparently left for the afterlife journey.

    3. c. 2400 BCE
      Abu Ghurob, Egypt

      Beekeeping carved in stone

      Reliefs in the sun temple of King Nyuserre show workers blowing smoke into horizontal hives and pouring honey into jars. Egyptians kept bees in stacked pipes of mud and clay.

    4. c. 900 BCE
      Tel Rehov, Jordan Valley

      The first apiary we can dig up

      Rows of unfired clay cylinder hives, some still holding wax and bee remains. Researchers estimate the site held around a hundred hives or more.

    5. 4th c. BCE
      Greece

      Aristotle watches the hive

      His History of Animals describes bee behaviour in detail, though he assumed the colony's leader was a king.

    6. 29 BCE
      Rome

      Virgil's beekeeping poem

      Book IV of the Georgics is a practical and poetic guide to keeping bees, from siting hives to handling swarms.

    7. Middle Ages
      Europe

      Skeps, monks and mead

      Woven straw skeps spread across Europe. Monasteries kept bees for church candles of beeswax and brewed honey into mead. Harvesting often meant destroying the colony.

    8. 1609
      England

      The king is a queen

      Charles Butler's The Feminine Monarchie argued that the colony's leader is female. Jan Swammerdam later confirmed it by dissection.

    9. 1622
      Virginia colony

      Honey bees reach North America

      European honey bees arrived with colonists. Before then the Americas had many native bees, but no honey bees.

    10. 1851
      Philadelphia, USA

      Bee space and the movable frame

      Lorenzo Langstroth noticed bees leave a gap of about 6–9 mm untouched. Hanging frames with exactly that spacing meant combs could be lifted out without killing the colony. He patented the hive in 1852.

    11. 1865
      Italy

      The centrifugal extractor

      Franz von Hruschka spun honey out of uncapped combs, so the empty comb could go back to the bees to refill.

    12. 1973
      Stockholm

      A Nobel for the dance

      Karl von Frisch shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for work including decoding the waggle dance.

    13. 2006
      United States

      Colony collapse disorder

      Beekeepers reported hives emptying almost overnight, and the problem got a name. Research since points to a mix of causes: varroa mites and the viruses they spread, pesticides and poor nutrition.

    14. 2018
      Worldwide

      The first World Bee Day

      The United Nations set 20 May, the birthday of Slovenian beekeeping pioneer Anton Janša, as a day to highlight pollinators.

    About a third of the food we eat depends to some degree on animal pollination.

    Honey bees get most of the attention, but most pollination is done by wild bees, flies, moths and beetles. Bumblebees buzz tomatoes into releasing pollen; mason bees work fruit blossom in cold spring weather when honey bees stay home. Planting a mix of flowers that bloom from early spring to late autumn helps all of them.

    Before you write

    Common questions

    There's a swarm of bees in my garden. What should I do?

    Stay calm and keep your distance. A swarm is a colony looking for a new home and the bees are full of honey and rarely sting. Don't spray or disturb them. Call a local beekeeping association or swarm collector; many will collect for free.

    Why has my honey gone solid?

    All real honey crystallizes eventually; it's the glucose forming crystals. Stand the jar in warm water (about 40 °C) and stir until it's runny. Avoid the microwave, which overheats it.

    Is raw honey safe for babies?

    No honey of any kind should be given to babies under 12 months, because of the risk of infant botulism.

    What does adopting a hive mean?

    You sponsor one colony for a year. You get photos and notes from inspections, a jar from your hive's harvest, and an invitation to an open day to meet them.

    Can I visit the apiary?

    Yes, on open days. We provide suits and veils. Wear long trousers and closed shoes, and skip perfume.
